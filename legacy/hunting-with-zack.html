    The chance index is a relative estimate built from typical seasonal patterns (dawn and dusk movement, rut timing, early-season heat, late-season cold, weather), not a measured probability. Times are Alberta Time (UTC−6), which Alberta uses year-round from Nov 1, 2026, so there is no fall-back hour. *Legal hours are ½ hour before sunrise to ½ hour after sunset (2026 Alberta Guide to Hunting Regulations). Shot opportunity turns sightings into a realistic chance of a shot at a legal animal: it starts from the WMU’s actual hunter success for your species, assumes about 7 days hunted per hunter and about 2 shot chances for every animal taken, is calibrated so an experienced drive-and-glass hunter in a WMU with 30% success has about a 25% chance of a shot on a typical day, then scales by how much of a typical day’s movement falls inside your outing (rut, weather, wind, pressure and moon included) and by your hunting style (driving and glassing ×3, still-hunting ×1.6) and experience (experienced ×1.8, very experienced ×2.5). It drops to zero when the season is closed, on closed Sundays, or when only draw seasons are open and you don’t hold a draw licence, and is cut for archery-only seasons. Treat it as a comparison between times and units, not a promise.

        07 · Tomorrow · Monday, Nov 2 · WMU 348 · Westmans
        Two tags at Westmans
        
  
        Trent and Zack's hunt at the grazing reserve, told the way we want it to go, using tomorrow's forecast, sun times and moon for WMU 348.
        
  
        
  
        7:50 am. The truck rolls to a stop at Westmans with the heater ticking and frost on every blade of grass. It's about −5 °C, no wind to speak of, and a light northwest breeze is forecast once the sun is up, so Trent and Zack come in from the southeast with it in their faces. The last-quarter moon, a little under half lit, hangs high in the south while they load up and walk the fence line to the aspen bluff above the pasture. They're set up and glassing with twenty minutes to spare.
        
  
        8:11 am · legal light. Grey light comes up over the reserve. It's a Monday in the first week of the general whitetail season, the gate is quiet, and nobody else is out. It's pre-rut, and the cold morning has the deer up and moving.
        
  
        8:26 am. Two does slip out of the willows and start feeding along the edge of the pasture. Zack taps the whitetail button on the hunt log. Neither of them says a word, because does in the open this early in November usually mean someone is following.
        
  
        8:39 am. He is. Just as the sun breaks the tree line, a heavy-necked buck crashes out of the willows after them, and the does scatter. Even at a run his rack is huge: tall tines, long beams, easily a 160-inch deer. He never stops. Zack swings with him across the open pasture at about 140 m, keeps the crosshairs a body length in front of his chest, and squeezes. It's a perfect lead through both lungs. The buck folds mid-stride and doesn't get up. Trent just says, "Did you see that?"
        
  
        Morning. Up close he's even bigger. They tag him on the spot, take plenty of photos with that rack, and keep evidence of sex attached. With a high of 8 °C coming, they skin and quarter him by noon, bag the meat and get it cooling in the shade. Then they eat lunch on the tailgate and grin a lot.
        
  
        4:30 pm. Now it's Trent's turn. The afternoon is calm and dry, and the light northwest breeze holds steady. He settles in at the corner of a poplar bluff overlooking the hay field, with the wind in his face and his rifle across his knees, and lets the reserve go quiet. Zack glasses from the truck down the road.
        
  
        5:48 pm. Twenty minutes before sunset, a full-grown 4-pointer steps out of the poplars up on the ridge above the hay field: deep body, grey face, four tines a side. He stands skylined along the top without a care. Trent has all the time he needs. He waits until the buck stops broadside at 150 m, settles the crosshairs tight behind the shoulder, and squeezes. It's a clean double-lung hit, and the buck goes down right there on the ridge.
        
  
        After the shot. They climb the ridge in the last of the light, tag him, and take photos with the sunset behind him. The best part is that it's all downhill from there. They have an easy drag down the slope, straight to the truck.
        
  
        6:37 pm · end of legal light. Both tags are filled, the truck is loaded, and the hunt log shows a full day of sightings.
        
  
        Westmans, WMU 348: a 160-inch runner and a mature 4-pointer. Complete success.
